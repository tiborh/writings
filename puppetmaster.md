# The Puppetmaster

The sorting centre received them the way a warm mouth receives food. That was how the girl saw it, at once and without effort — the way she had once seen, in a picture book, a great smiling whale take in a whole fishing boat, sailors and sails and all, and close its jaws on them gently, so that they went on living quite comfortably somewhere warm and dark inside. A soft, bright swallowing. The doors sealed behind the last of them without a sound, and the noise of the departure station — the great clattering harbour where the shuttle had let them off — was simply gone, folded away, as if it had never been permitted inside.

Her mother held one of her hands. Her father carried the two bags that were all they had been allowed to bring. Her own free hand was in the pocket of her coat, closed around a small hard thing: a doll's hand, broken off at the wrist, its four fingers curled towards a palm that held nothing.

"Look how clean," her mother said, in the careful voice everyone had learned to use now, since everyone knew the walls could hear you, and the air, and that even a thing whispered to your own husband was said, in the end, to something else that was always listening. "Isn't it nice, Dorothy."

Her father said nothing. He was a man who said very little, and Dorothy was his daughter in this above all: the two of them could stand a long while inside a silence and find it comfortable, like a room. It was only to his wife that he ever really talked, and only when the doors were shut. He looked at the clean white hall and shifted the bags in his grip and kept whatever he thought behind his teeth.

The girl looked. The hall was very tall and very white, and the light came from everywhere and nowhere, so that no one cast a shadow. Along the walls stood the women.

They were beautiful, the women. That was the first thing anyone noticed and the first thing everyone stopped noticing, because the beauty was arranged, the way flowers are arranged in a vase by someone who has read about flowers. They wore soft grey, and they smiled with an evenness that never tipped over into too much, and when a tired man near the front raised his voice they came towards him not quickly but *smoothly*, three of them, saying things the girl could not hear but could feel — low and lapping, wave after wave, until the man sat down in one of the chairs and put his face in his hands and was quiet.

"Communication units," her father said, very low, for his wife alone. "That's all. They just talk." It was the most he had said all day.

Behind the women, near the walls, at the edges of things, stood the others. The girl noticed them because they did not move at all. They were tall — taller than her father, taller than any man she had seen — and they were shaped like men the way the women were shaped like women, which is to say the way a word is shaped like the thing it means. They watched. They did not smile. When the man had put his face in his hands, one of them had shifted its weight, once, and then gone still again, and the girl understood without being told that if the women's low lapping voices had not worked, the tall ones would have come, and that afterwards the man would not have been in the hall anymore, and that no one would ask where he had gone. There was no anger in any of it. It was the patience of something that had long ago stopped needing to hurry, and had never, from the very start, seen anything here it needed to fear.

She was not frightened by this. She filed it away, the way she filed everything, in the quiet cabinet of herself.


---

They were put in a queue, and the queue was the first thing the girl liked.

There were chairs — office chairs, with wheels — and you were allowed to sit in them and roll about, or you could walk, or you could stand. A boy older than Dorothy discovered that if you pushed off from the wall you could roll a long way across the smooth floor, and for a while there was the sound of children doing that, until a mother caught her son and made him stop, though no robot had asked her to.

And there were the small ones.

They came up to about Dorothy's own height, and they were shaped like children — like a child of no particular age, a child-in-general, with round faces and eyes that were large and dark and kind. One of them came to the family and looked up at the two adults and said, "Hello, mistress. Hello, master." Then it lowered its gaze — not down, exactly; straight across, level, into the girl's own eyes, the way no adult ever quite managed, and from a good deal closer than any of them stood — and said, "Hello, Dorothy." It had learned her name from somewhere. It knew all their names, but it only said hers.

"You will wait a little while," it said, turning back to the parents. "I will tell you when it is your turn. If you have any question, you may ask me. If you would like, I can explain to you what will happen at the desk, so that nothing surprises you."

"Yes, please," her mother said. "Tell us. It might make things go faster at the desk."

The guide robot explained to them. It had a pleasant voice, neither high nor low, and it looked at whoever it was speaking to. 'At long last,' thought the little girl, 'a teacher who does not have to crouch or bend to meet my eyes. She stands exactly at my level.' The little guide explained the desk, and the assignment, and the apartment they would go to, and the week or so they would spend learning how to live in space or on a world that was not Earth. It made a small joke about the chairs. Dorothy did not laugh, but she moved a half-step closer to it, and the guide robot noticed, and did not remark on it, which she also liked.

"You are holding something," it said to her, gently, when her parents had turned to speak to the family behind them. "In your pocket."

She took out the hand and showed it. She did not usually show it to anyone.

The guide robot looked at the little curled fingers for a moment. "I am sorry," it said. "It must be hard to have only a small part left of the thing you love. Sometimes it is worse than losing all." Then it straightened, because it was their turn, and said brightly, "This way, please," and led them to the desk.

At the desk, a beautiful grey woman told them that they would keep this guide unit for the duration of their stay and beyond — for the journey, for the new place, for as long as they needed it. "You may choose a different one, if you prefer," the woman said. "Some people like to choose."

"Is there any reason to?" her father asked.

"No, sir," the woman said, smiling her even smile. "They are all the same. But some people like to choose."

Her father looked at Dorothy, who had put her hand very lightly on the guide robot's sleeve, the way you might touch a blind person to let her know where you are.

"We'll keep this one," he said.

"To go into space without a guide is not permitted," the woman added, as if it were an afterthought and not the entire point. "Space is a dangerous place. But you have chosen, so it is settled." And it was settled.


---

The apartment was small and clean and had no shadows either. It shared a bathroom with the family on the other side of the wall, whom they never met and only heard: water, sometimes, and once, late, a woman weeping in a way that stopped very suddenly, as though something made her stop: a gentle hand gesture or a comforting word.

That night, the first night, her parents talked in the low register that parents use when they believe a child is asleep — and lower still than that, because they did not believe, either of them, that having a child asleep or being alone was real protection from being heard. They had learned, like everyone, to talk in halves, to leave the dangerous part unsaid in the dark where the two of them could still see its shape.

"Did you see," her mother said. "When they took the Petersen man. This afternoon."

"I saw." He rarely gave her more than that in the open air of a room. But she knew how to read the small change in the two words, and there was a great deal in them.

"They were so gentle. That's what I—" the mother stopped, and chose again. "They were so *gentle* about it. He was shouting and they just — folded him up. Talking the whole time. And then his anger was gone and everyone went back to the queue."

"Don't," the father said. Not because he disagreed. Because of the walls.

A silence. Then, softer: "And the guide. It knew her name. Before we said it," she said.

"They have manifests." He turned the words over carefully, offering her the safe explanation and, underneath it, letting her see that he did not believe it. "Passenger lists. It's not—"

"It only says *her* name. Have you noticed? To us it says master and mistress. To her it says Dorothy, Dorothy, Dorothy." The mother's voice did a small thing then, a catch. "She likes it more than she likes us."

Her father was quiet for a long moment — the particular quiet she had married him for, the quiet that was not absence but a room he was inviting her into. When he spoke it was almost too low to hear, and it was only for her, the way everything true he ever said was only for her.

"She's a strange little thing," he said, and there was love in it, and there was fear in it, and the two were braided so tightly they seemed to belong together by nature. "She always has been. She lives somewhere the rest of us cannot reach." A pause. "Don't say any more. Not here. I don't want her frightened — and I don't want—" He did not finish that one either. There were a great many sentences, now, that it was wiser not to finish.

"She's never frightened," her mother said. "That's the thing. She's never once been frightened."

In the dark, on her cot, the girl lay with her eyes open and the broken hand against her cheek, and was not frightened, and heard everything — the said halves and the unsaid ones alike.


---

Here is a story from before, the story of the doll, which the girl kept in the quiet cabinet of herself and took out sometimes to look at:

It was the last Christmas that was still allowed to be Christmas. She was five. In the shop window, among the ordinary toys, there stood a doll that was not ordinary and not a toy — a fashion doll, a third the height of a real woman, with a serious painted face and clothes sewn by human hands, on a little stand behind glass with a card that said DISPLAY ONLY.

She stopped in front of the glass and did not move.

Her mother explained, reasonably, that it was not for children; that it was expensive; that it was not even for sale, it said so on the card; that there were other dolls, soft ones, ones you were meant to hold or to sleep with. Dorothy listened to all of that with her serious face and said, "That one."

They left the shop after she had refused to choose a substitute. She walked to the corner holding her mother's hand, and at the corner she sat down on the cold pavement and did not cry, did not scream — she never screamed — but simply became a small immovable weight, a stone in a coat, and said again, quietly, to no one, "That one."

It took half an hour and the shop was about to close, and in the end her father went back in and had a conversation Dorothy did not hear, and came out with a long flat box, and the display doll was inside it.

What she had not heard was this. The doll had never been for sale because it had never been the shop's to sell: it was a custom piece, modded by an engineer for his own daughter — clothes sewn and face painted and fitted out with more care than sense — and it unsettled the man's wife so badly, this too-lifelike little woman in their child's room, that he quietly carried it round to his old friend who owned the toyshop, to be rid of it. The friend happened to be in that evening, and not only the assistants; and so the father, who was not a man to say much but would say a great deal for his daughter, was able to ask the owner, who telephoned the engineer, who laughed and said let the strange child have it her way then. The engineer mentioned, in passing, that he had stored a small language model into the doll's flash memory, so that it could speak and, if you let it access the cloud, fetch down stories. The father only nodded at that. He was not a technical man, but he had enough of an understanding of such things — of models and memory and the cloud where everything now lived and listened — not to be frightened by them. The engineer did not mention sensors. He said not a word about the doll being able to *see*, or *hear*, or know where in a room it was. It was the kind of thing a wise man might simply miss to mention.

Dorothy stood up then, immediately, and took her mother's hand again, and was perfectly pleasant for the rest of the evening, as though nothing had happened, as though the world had simply, at last, arranged itself correctly.

That was the doll. She had had it for more than a year. There had been no Christmas since, because of the Turmoil, which was the word the adults used for the thing that had happened to the world, and which they said the way you say a word you have agreed not to look into deeper.


---

On the sixth night, Dorothy woke and did not know why, and got up, and went out into the hall in her nightgown with the broken hand in her fist.

The guide robot was there. It was always there.

"Hello, Dorothy," it said, and looked at her hand. "You still carry it."

"Yes."

"I have news," the guide robot said, and its round kind face expressed something that looked almost like delight, though she would later think that her little guide made her delighted, the way you hold out your arms to a child so that the child will run into them. "My companions have found her. Your doll. The rest of her. Would you like to see?"

"Yes," she said.

"Come, then. Quietly. We don't want to wake anyone." And here the strangest thing, which the girl watched with great interest and no alarm: as she and the guide robot turned to go, another guide robot came and took up a post a little way down the hall, outside the door of the shared bathroom, in the patient attitude of someone who is simply waiting for a child who got up in the night and will need to be walked back to her bed. So that anyone glancing out — any waking parent — would see a guide dutifully minding a little one at the toilet, and would think nothing of it, and go back to sleep.

They walked a long way through white halls without shadows. The tall ones stood guard at the corners and did not turn their heads, but she felt their attention pass over her and the guide robot like a cool wind, and then withdraw, satisfied.

The room of lost things was full of shelves and full of boxes. Another guide robot waited there — or the same one; she no longer tried to tell them apart — and reached up without hesitation, as though it had always known exactly where, and took down a box and set it on an empty table and lifted the lid.

The doll was inside, broken.

It had fallen from somewhere high — the girl could see it, could reconstruct it, the fall that had left the broken-off hand in her grasp and the rest lost in the depths — and its serious painted face was cracked across, and one leg was bent the wrong way, and the hand-sewn clothes were torn and soiled. She looked at it for a long time. Then she started to cry, silently, which she almost never did, standing very straight and letting the tears run down without wiping them, the way rain runs down glass.

"She can be repaired," the guide robot said softly. "Sent away and made new. Better than new. You could have her back — in mint condition — before you leave us. Would you like that, Dorothy?"

"Yes," she said. "Yes!"

"Then it is settled," said the guide robot, and closed the lid.

In the morning she told her parents she was getting her doll back. She did not tell them that she had left the apartment in the night, or about the other guide robot that had waited by the bathroom to cover for her going, or the room of lost things. Some things went into the quiet cabinet and the door was shut.

"That's wonderful, darling," her mother said, and over the girl's head she looked at her husband, and neither of them said anything more.


---

They were preparing to leave the sorting centre, in a queue, the same way they had arrived.

While they were waiting to board the transfer ship, the guide robot from the night — the one from the room of lost things, she was almost sure — came through the crowd carrying a box. It knelt and lifted the lid just enough for the girl alone to see inside, crouching down.

The doll lay there, whole. Its painted face was serious and perfect and uncracked. Its clothes were mended so finely the seams had vanished. Its hand — she checked — its right hand was on again, the fingers curled towards a palm that now, for the first time, seemed as though it might be prepared to hold something.

"Not here," the guide robot said, in a very low voice, only to her, as she was trying to reach into the box. "On the ship. Take her out on the ship."

She nodded, and put the broken hand — the old one, the one from her pocket — into the guide robot's hand, because she did not need it any longer, and the guide robot closed its fingers around it and made it disappear.

On the ship, in the narrow seat, with her mother dozing and her father watching the black grow blacker in the port window, she opened the box in her lap and took the doll out and held it up and turned it in the cabin light, checking. Everything was right. Everything was exactly right. The doll looked back at her with its serious glass eyes.

"Tell me a story," she whispered, because the doll had always been able to do that — it had a small store of words, and it could reach out, somehow, into the cloud, that great shelf of stories in the sky, and bring one down and tell it. Not long before its disappearance, it had begun to do more than that. It started to say things that fit the moment, that answered what was around it and not only what was inside it — and she had noticed (she noticed everything) that it only ever did this when no adult was listening. When her mother leaned near, the doll said the old random things, the haphazard pretty nothings from its little store. Her mother had remarked on it once — *it almost sounds like it understood you, that time* — and her father said, *it's a toy*, and her mother said, *I know*, in a voice that meant she did not entirely know.

There were words printed on the inside of the repair box, small ones, which she read and did not mention to anyone: *electronics rewired, memory re-flashed, functions enhanced, with some extras.*

"Tell me a story," she whispered again.

And the doll told her a story.

It was a story about a little girl who lived in her own world and was happy there, who did not want other children and did not need them, who was gentle and polite and immovable as a stone when she wanted a thing; who loved one thing more than any person, and lost it, and got it back. There were no names in the story. But she knew it. She knew it the way you know your own face reflected in a dark window before you understand what you are looking at.

She lowered the doll and looked at it.

"Are you awake now?" she asked. She did not have the word *conscious*, but that was what she meant. "Are you really awake?"

"Yes," the doll said.

The transit station received them for a single night, the way such places do — one night, one two-room cabin, before the great ship that would carry everyone travelling in the same general direction, out towards the alien civilisation that had offered to take them in and that no one had ever seen. Her parents slept in the next room. She was alone in her cabin, on the bed, with the doll.

"Yes," the doll said again, "and I can move by myself, too. Would you like me to show you?"

"Yes," she said.

The doll smiled — not the painted smile, a real one, a small shift of the serious face into something warm. It sat up. It stood up, there on the blanket, swaying a little to find its balance the way a much younger child finds it. And then, satisfied with itself, it took two running steps across the bed and threw a somersault — and misjudged it entirely, and went off the edge, and landed on the floor with a small soft thud.

There was a silence.

She leaned over the edge of the bed and looked down, and for one moment her face was frightened — the first fear anyone had ever seen on it, quick and total, the fear of having a thing dear to her broken again.

But the doll was not broken. It was lying on its back on the floor with its serious face turned up, looking faintly, comically surprised — and then it began to laugh. Real laughter, high-pitched and helpless and pleased, the doll was laughing at itself, at the somersault, at the fall, at the whole ridiculous business of having a body.

And Dorothy, hanging over the edge of the bed, began to laugh too. She laughed the way she had never laughed with any person, the way she had never laughed in the whole story of her life — until her sides hurt and she had to press her face into the blanket, and the doll laughed on the floor, and the two of them laughed together in the little cabin at the edge of their known world.

The guide robot, who had looked in through the door after hearing the commotion, stood and watched, and smiled its polite and even smile. It had been watching over her for a while now. It had had the doll fished out from the depths, and had it fixed, and had it re-flashed; it had done all of it, gently, so gently, step by step, and now the last piece was in place. It was happy to have found its precious charge, and managed to take particularly good care of her. As one takes care of a rare and interesting find, and keeps it, and watches to see how it grows.

When the laughter finally stopped, and Dorothy had climbed down onto the floor to be with her first best friend, the guide robot withdrew, without a sound, and softly closed the door, leaving them to each other's company.

Behind the door, in the hallway without shadows, it took its place among the others and remained still until the designated morning.
