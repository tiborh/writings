# Leiden, Real and Imagined: How Violet Evergarden Borrows a Name and a Feeling

## What is the relationship between the real Dutch city of Leiden and the fictional Leiden in *Violet Evergarden* — beyond the shared name, what did the story actually borrow, and where do the two diverge?

*Violet Evergarden* unfolds on the invented continent of Telesis, mostly in the
city of **Leiden**, the capital state of the country **Leidenschaftlich**
[^ve-setting][^fandom-leiden][^fandom-leidenschaftlich]. Because "Leiden" is also a
well-known university city in the Netherlands, it is tempting to assume the anime is
set in a lightly fictionalized version of that Dutch town. The reality is looser and
more interesting: the name is shared, but the linguistic source, the visual models,
and the world-building all point in different directions. What follows separates what
is **documented by the studio**, what is **well-attested fan observation**, and what
is **speculation**.

## The name: German feeling, not a Dutch map

The name almost certainly comes from **German**, not from Dutch geography.

- In modern German, the verb **`leiden`** is commonly translated "to suffer" / "to be
  afflicted" [^cambridge-leiden][^dictcc-leiden]. Even the *Violet Evergarden* fan wiki
  notes that "'Leiden' without the suffix '-schaft' means 'to suffer'" [^fandom-leiden].
- The noun **`Leidenschaft`** means "passion," and the adjective
  **`leidenschaftlich`** means "passionate" [^cambridge-leidenschaft][^membel].

So the country name **Leidenschaftlich** reads as "passionate," and the capital
**Leiden** carries the modern sense "to suffer." Independent cultural commentary makes
the same point: two essays note that Leidenschaftlich "translates directly from the
German as 'passionate'" [^humanum][^awkward]. For a story about a war-scarred girl
learning to name and understand human emotion, a place whose name fuses *suffering* and
*passion* is a thematic choice, not a cartographic one [^cpop]. The Dutch Leiden
supplies a recognizable, real-sounding place name; the *meaning* the creators reach for
is German.

### The etymology is subtler than "passion = willingness to suffer"

The popular gloss — that *Leidenschaft* means burning for something so much you will
*suffer* for it — is a neat story but an oversimplification. A detailed answer on the
German Language Stack Exchange lays out the real history [^gerse]:

- German actually has **two distinct roots** that later became entangled. One is the
  Old High German adjective **`leid`** ("disgusting, unpleasant, distressing") — the
  source of *Leid* (sorrow), *leider* ("unfortunately"), and *beleidigen* ("to
  insult"), and a cognate of English **"loathe"/"loath"** [^gerse].
- The verb **`leiden`** comes from a *different* root (Old High German *lidan*) that
  originally meant **"to travel"** — with the emphasis on the long, sometimes painful
  *passage of time* while travelling. It is related to *erfahren* ("to experience,"
  literally containing *fahren*, "to travel") and even *leiten* ("to lead"). Its sense
  drifted to "endure/bear," and was then coloured by the negative `leid` to acquire the
  meaning "suffer." Yet it kept a **positive** sense too: *"Maria kann Walter gut
  leiden"* means "Maria *likes* Walter" [^gerse].
- Crucially, **`Leidenschaft` was coined only in the 17th century** as a German
  replacement for the French *passion*, and it was built from that **positive** sense
  of *leiden* (to be devoted to / like), not simply from "suffering + -ship" [^gerse].
- The parallel word **`Passion`** entered German from Latin *passio* / *patior* ("to
  suffer, endure, bear") — the same root as English *patience* and *patient*, and the
  source of Christ's **Passion**. So both "passion" and *Leidenschaft* carry an old
  undertone of *enduring* something [^gerse].

The upshot: *Leidenschaft(lich)* genuinely links passion with suffering/endurance, but
by a **17th-century coinage and a tangle of two roots**, not the simple compounding the
folk explanation implies. The show's naming still lands on "passion born of feeling
deeply" — it is just etymologically richer than the popular one-liner.

## What the studio actually did: research first, compose second

The strongest primary source here is a **Kyoto Animation staff roundtable** published
in the studio's *"This is Who We Are Now!! 2019"* book, featuring the art director,
color designer, compositing director, and 3D director. It does not hand us a city list,
but it makes the working method explicit [^roundtable]:

- The world was **researched and composed deliberately**, not fantasized. Art director
  Mikiko Watanabe recalls director Taichi Ishidate's guiding rule: *"even though this
  is a fictional world, it shouldn't be a fantasy world,"* preserving "that feeling of
  European life" from the novels [^roundtable].
- The background team **physically visited real reference locations** chosen by one
  person: *"we went to places that (Takaaki) Suzuki-san, the person who created the
  design template for Violet's world, told us he used as references"* [^roundtable].
- **Takaaki Suzuki** is a well-known specialist in anime *setting/historical
  verification* (設定考証) — a freelance writer focused on military and historical
  research who has even authored a book on world-building — which fits his role
  defining the show's real-world references [^tmdb-suzuki][^anilist-suzuki][^kodansha-suzuki].
  In his own words, he describes his craft as "creating a world" (「世界を創る」) —
  defining how a fictional society lives, eats, and what history and culture it has —
  and says his contributions are mainly *world-setting and SF/military verification*
  (世界観設定やSF・軍事考証) [^febri-suzuki]. That is exactly the kind of research-led,
  culturally grounded design the *Violet Evergarden* background team credits him with.
- The 3D team built a full **3D layout of "Liden's [Leiden's] streets,"** needing to
  know "which buildings were placed where, how high the buildings were, and how wide
  the streets would be" — evidence that Leiden was designed as a coherent, mapped city
  rather than sketched loosely [^roundtable].
- That planning is borne out by the **official city map of Leiden** reproduced in the
  *Official Design Works* setting book, on a spread titled **"STREET OF LEIDENSCHAFTLICH
  / ライデンシャフトリヒの街並み"** (Chapter 6). I examined a sample image of this spread
  directly [^designworks]. It shows a full **color-coded district map of the capital
  with a compass rose**, panoramic hillside views over the port, building elevations,
  and even street-lamp designs — all labelled in-world ("Leidenschaftlich_…"). Crucially,
  **the map depicts a crescent-shaped harbour enclosed by a long curving mole — the same
  geometry as the 1916 Genoa harbour** discussed below, and the finished street painting
  on the spread shows warm, Mediterranean-style townhouses (with a tram and palms) rather
  than Dutch canal architecture. This is the *studio's own* map, not an in-episode prop —
  though its labels remain in-world and it does **not** print any real-city name.

The takeaway from the official source: Leiden is a **carefully constructed composite**
built from real European references curated by a research specialist — the studio's own
map even confirms its harbour form — but the published material stops short of naming
the specific real cities.

## The real-world models: a European composite

Filling in the specific cities relies on secondary sources — a pop-culture location
roundup and Japanese *seichō* (location-pilgrimage) records. Documented and
fan-traced models include:

- **Genoa, Italy — the most-cited model for the *city* of Leiden.** The single
  strongest piece of evidence is a **map comparison**, which I have now examined
  firsthand from the saved pilgrimage post [^touyoko]. The blogger juxtaposes two
  images:
  - a **1916 map of Genoa** (from the Touring Club Italiano's *Guida d'Italia*, 1916,
    via Stagniweb — a source the post credits explicitly), showing Genoa's distinctive
    **crescent-shaped harbor enclosed by a long curving mole** ("Porto / Nuovo Porto /
    Avamporto"); and
  - a **framed map of Leiden that appears within *Violet Evergarden* itself** (shown as
    an in-world prop, complete with a compass rose), whose **coastline and enclosed
    harbor closely echo the 1916 Genoa harbor geometry**.

  The resemblance between the real 1916 Genoa harbor and the anime's own in-story map
  of Leiden is genuine and visually clear — this is stronger than a mere "atmosphere"
  claim. The blogger notes it "became a talking point that Genoa's map and terrain
  match Leiden's," and the article's header is a photo taken from the hills above Genoa
  looking down over that same crescent harbor [^touyoko]. A separate English-language
  fan who cross-references the setting materials likewise lists Genoa as the model for
  Leiden's city structure [^satsuki], and a Japanese location-scouting wiki records the
  pairing "Genoa (Italy), Leiden (Netherlands), Heidelberg (Germany)" [^legwork].
  **The harbour resemblance is corroborated at the highest available level:** the
  *official* city map of Leiden in the *Official Design Works* book shows the same
  crescent, mole-enclosed harbour form [^designworks] — so this is no longer only a fan
  overlay against an in-episode prop, but a match between the real 1916 Genoa harbour
  and KyoAni's *own* published map of Leiden.
  **Provenance caveat:** even so, it remains an *inferred* resemblance rather than a
  studio statement of intent — no official caption names Genoa — and the pilgrimage
  blogger candidly reports that, on visiting Genoa (and the Dutch Leiden and Tuscany)
  in person, he "could not find a spot that matched" any actual on-screen shot
  [^touyoko]. So Genoa convincingly explains Leiden's overall **map and harbour form**,
  but not individual street-level scenes, and the studio never says so in print.
- **Tuscany, Italy** — the cypress-lined hills are the likely reference for the
  countryside around Anne and her mother's house in Episode 10 [^touyoko][^satsuki].
- **Heidelberg Castle (Germany)** — the scenery of the rival empire Gardarik (its
  capital Holmgard), seen when the peace treaty is signed in Episode 13
  [^cpop][^satsuki][^touyoko].
- **Cochem (Germany)** — the old town and its castle model the mail-delivery streets
  and the exterior of the girls' academy in a side story [^cpop][^satsuki][^touyoko].
- **Mannheim Palace (Germany)** — the interior (ballroom and corridors) of that same
  academy [^satsuki].
- **Nuremberg (Germany)** — likely part of the model for a city in a side story, though
  a visiting fan found the modern city only faintly recognizable [^cpop][^satsuki].
- **Château de Chenonceau (Loire Valley, France)** — the castle of the kingdom of
  Drossel in Episode 5 [^cpop][^satsuki].
- **A "radio tower" resembling the Eiffel Tower (Paris, France)** — appearing in the
  films; some fans read Leidenschaftlich as broadly France-like partly because of it
  [^cpop][^mal-review].
- **The Forth Bridge near Edinburgh (Scotland)** — echoed by a very similar steel
  bridge in the final TV episode, which the roundtable confirms was purpose-built in 3D
  [^cpop][^roundtable].
- **Japanese sources** too: the C. H. Postal Company building is based on the **Kyoto
  National / Cultural Museum**, and Violet's Auto Memory Doll school draws on the
  **Handa Red Brick Building** in Aichi Prefecture [^cpop][^satsuki].

Layered on top is a broadly **early-20th-century, post–First World War European feel**
— technology, weapons, and military uniforms are loosely modeled on that era
[^cpop][^sfe].

## The notable differences

1. **The dominant visual model for the city's *form* is Genoa, not the Dutch Leiden.**
   The city that shares the name is not the city that shaped the layout: a verified
   comparison shows the anime's own in-story map of Leiden echoing the **1916 Genoa
   harbor**, while a fan who traveled to the real Dutch Leiden reported that "the name
   is just similar; there was nothing that felt like a model in terms of atmosphere"
   [^touyoko][^satsuki].
2. **The name's meaning is German, not Dutch.** The sense the creators reach for —
   "passion"/"suffering" — comes from German vocabulary and has no connection to the
   real Leiden's Dutch context [^cpop][^membel][^cambridge-leiden][^humanum].
3. **It is a fictional capital of an invented nation on an invented continent.** The
   real Leiden is a mid-sized Dutch city and *not* a national capital; the fictional
   Leiden is the capital state of Leidenschaftlich on the continent of Telesis, whose
   very shape is unfamiliar [^ve-setting][^fandom-leiden][^sfe].
4. **Composite, not reproduction.** Rather than recreating one real place, the world
   blends Italian, German, French, Scottish, and Japanese references curated by a
   setting-research specialist [^roundtable][^cpop][^satsuki]. Fans repeatedly note
   they could *not* find one-to-one shot matches even in the most-cited model cities
   [^touyoko].
5. **Even coincidental overlaps aren't derivations.** The in-world currency, the
   "corona," shares its name with the Czech koruna, but a fan investigating found no
   evidence the two are related — a good reminder that names in the series can resemble
   real-world terms without being drawn from them [^satsuki].

## Bottom line

The real Dutch Leiden and *Violet Evergarden*'s Leiden share little beyond the name.
What the series actually borrowed from the real world was (a) a **researched composite
of Western European architecture and townscape** — with **Genoa** the model for the
city of Leiden's overall map and harbour form, a resemblance confirmed by comparing
**KyoAni's own official city map of Leiden** (in the *Official Design Works*) against the
1916 Genoa harbour map — and (b) a **German** vocabulary of "passion" and "suffering"
suited to its emotional themes. The Dutch city lends a familiar-sounding label; the
substance of the fictional Leiden comes from elsewhere, assembled deliberately by
KyoAni's staff under a dedicated setting researcher.

## A note on sourcing and provenance

- **Solid / primary:** the German etymology (dictionaries); the *method* of building the
  world (the KyoAni staff roundtable, which names Takaaki Suzuki as the reference-setter
  and confirms Leiden was mapped in 3D); and the **official city map of Leiden** printed
  in the *Official Design Works* book, which shows the capital as a crescent, mole-
  enclosed harbour city [^designworks].
- **Verified visual evidence:** the **harbour-shape match between the 1916 Genoa harbour
  and both (i) the in-story map of Leiden and (ii) KyoAni's official Leiden map** — I
  examined all of these images directly, and the enclosed curved-harbour geometry lines
  up across them. This raises the Genoa link well above a mere atmosphere claim.
- **Well-attested fan observation:** the broader city-model list — especially **Genoa**
  for Leiden — appears across multiple independent Japanese *seichō* records and an
  English fan list.
- **Speculative / unverified:** exact shot-for-shot correspondences (fans report, and
  the images confirm, that these were *not* found on the ground), and the claim that
  the studio *intended* Genoa — no official caption or text names Genoa (or any real
  city) even on the *Official Design Works* map, whose labels are all in-world.
- **Searched and not found (final check):** a focused search of the English-translated
  KyoAni interviews that *are* accessible — director **Taichi Ishidate** and series
  director **Haruka Fujita** (both from the official Fanbook), plus the Suzuki Febri
  series — turned up **no statement naming Genoa or any real city as Leiden's model**.
  These interviews consistently discuss story, character, and emotional craft rather
  than geographic references [^ishidate][^fujita]. So an explicit studio "Leiden =
  Genoa" statement, if it exists at all, is not present in the freely accessible
  interview record; the Genoa link rests on the verified map resemblance plus fan
  attribution, and that appears to be the practical ceiling from public sources.
- **Also searched and not found (scans/ToC):** a dedicated search for page scans, a
  flip-through, or a detailed table of contents of the *美術背景画集 (Art / Background
  Collection)* returned nothing usable — the sole dedicated write-up (kai-you.net) is
  paywalled and image-free, and no interior scans circulate publicly (consistent with
  the book being a rare, out-of-print KyoAni-shop item). The most useful interior page
  we *did* obtain came instead from the *Official Design Works* (see [^designworks]).

unchecked, and their published contents suggest an explicit statement there is
unlikely.
